# Bones on the Arctic Shore: Zooarchaeology, Spatial Patterning, and Early Modern Whaling at Gåshamna, Hornsund, Svalbard
Youri van den Hurk – University Museum, University of Bergen, Norway
Matthias Lang –

# Abstract
Coastal archaeological sites preserve exceptional evidence for early modern resource extraction, but their exposed surface assemblages are affected by erosion and redeposition. This paper presents an integrated zooarchaeological, osteometric, radiocarbon, and spatial analysis of the faunal remains from Gåshamna West, Hornsund, Svalbard, a historically documented shore-based whaling locality. The assemblage is dominated by bowhead whale (Balaena mysticetus), including 49 crania and postcranial material. Sawn mandibles and chopped vertebral elements indicate systematic carcass processing. Spatial analysis shows that whale remains form structured concentrations around tryworks/blubber ovens and adjacent gravel terraces, preserving the broad organization of carcass landing, flensing, head processing, disarticulation, and discard. Osteometric and fusion evidence suggests a strong focus on adult or near-adult bowheads, although juvenile individuals are also represented. Radiocarbon dating supports an early seventeenth century date, consistent with the documented bay-whaling phase, while highlighting complexities caused by marine reservoir effects and element-specific offsets in tympanic bullae. Non-cetacean remains, including cattle, polar bear, reindeer, Arctic fox, walrus, and seal, demonstrate later occupation by Norwegian trappers, scavenger activity, and natural accumulation. Gåshamna West is therefore best understood as a multi-period Arctic palimpsest rather than a single-phase whaling deposit.

# Introduction
Svalbard has no indigenous population and no continuous pre-modern settlement history; instead, its cultural record is dominated by short, repeated episodes of seasonal or overwintering exploitation by externally based groups. From the early seventeenth century, the archipelago became a focal arena for European commercial whaling and associated hunting, followed later by Russian Pomor overwintering hunts and, from the nineteenth century onward, Norwegian trapping and scientific activity. Together these phases produced a distinctive archaeological record in which industrial processing features, ephemeral dwellings, graves, and large faunal assemblages occur in direct association with a historically documented, international extractive economy (Conway, 1906; Hacquebord, 2001).
Within this broader setting, Hornsund—the southernmost major fjord system on Spitsbergen—was repeatedly used as a bay-whaling landscape during the early modern period. Bay-whaling targeted bowhead whales (Balaena mysticetus) entering fjords and nearshore waters, with carcasses flensed and rendered at shore stations where blubber was converted into oil in brick-built tryworks (“blubber ovens”). Such stations leave a characteristic archaeological signature: oven/furnace remains, working platforms, dwellings or tent foundations, graves, and extensive whale bone scatters and concentrations. In Svalbard overall, the density of whaling stations and bone deposits documents intensive exploitation of bowheads and other marine mammals, with long-term ecological consequences (Hacquebord, 2001).
Gåshamna is one of the key cultural-heritage localities in Hornsund for investigating the relationship between historically described bay-whaling and the archaeological record. The site is located on the southern side of Hornsund, in Sørkapp Land, as an open bay between Schønningholmane and Hornstullodden. Its archaeological deposits occur on an active gravel-plain/deltaic landform (Gåshamnøyra) shaped by meltwater and shifting channels, creating an unusually dynamic taphonomic setting in which erosion and redeposition continuously expose and remove cultural material. This combination—dense early modern industrial remains, large faunal assemblages, and rapid geomorphic change—makes Gåshamna both exceptionally informative and unusually vulnerable.
Despite several surveys and emergency excavations, the zooarchaeological component of Gåshamna has remained under-analysed relative to the scale of the surface assemblage and its potential to address core questions in early Arctic industry: which cetacean and non-cetacean taxa are represented at processing sites; how many individuals were brought to shore; what age classes were exploited; whether carcass transport and butchery followed standardized operational routines; and how whalers provisioned themselves during seasonal occupation. Beyond the early modern European whaling phase, Gåshamna also offers an opportunity to examine what traces remain of later activity in Svalbard, including possible use of the site by Pomors, Norwegian trappers, and other subsequent visitors or occupants. This paper addresses these issues through an integrated analysis of the exposed faunal assemblage and associated site structure at Gåshamna West, combining zooarchaeological analysis, osteometrics, radiocarbon dating, and spatial analysis with the documentary and archaeological research history of Hornsund whaling.

# Location, landscape setting, and site structure
Gåshamna is a bay on the southern shore of Hornsund in Sørkapp Land (southern Spitsbergen). The shoreline and adjacent plain are dominated by unconsolidated gravels associated with the Gåshamnøyra gravel plain between Gåshamna and the glacier Gåsbreen. In practical archaeological terms, this setting produces two crucial conditions: (1) exceptional exposure and visibility of bone and structural remains on the surface, and (2) high rates of loss and displacement as meltwater reworks sediments and channels migrate.
Archaeological remains in Gåshamna are typically described as forming two main loci—Gåshamna East and Gåshamna West—separated by the glacial river draining Gåsbreen. Both loci contain features consistent with early modern shore-based whaling infrastructure (tryworks/blubber ovens, working areas, dwellings/tent foundations) and associated bone concentrations, alongside evidence for later reuse of the landscape by other groups.
Written sources place Hornsund within the competitive sphere of early seventeenth-century European whaling, including episodes of conflict and negotiated access among English and Dutch actors, followed by changing patterns of use as whaling shifted to pelagic whaling abandoning shore-based whaling. (Purchas, 1906; Conway, 1906; Hacquebord, 1984). Gåshamna is plausibly identifiable with “Bowles Bay” in this historical literature; the Norwegian Polar Data Centre’s place-name registry notes the long-standing debate and records Conway’s (1906) interpretation of Bowles Bay as Gåshamna. Archaeologically, the extensive whale bone deposits and multiple tryworks features fit the expectations of an intensively used bay-whaling station, comparable in functional terms (though not in scale) to better-known sites elsewhere in Svalbard, such as Smeerenburg.
After the decline of intensive shore-based whaling, Pomor activity became an important component of Svalbard’s cultural landscape, including in the Hornsund region, dating to the 18th–mid-19th century. The nearby Schønningholmane site has been treated as a Pomor settlement complex (Chochorowski & Jasiński, 1988), providing a plausible regional context for Pomor land use and resource harvesting in the vicinity of Gåshamna.
Norwegian trappers in Hornsund reused earlier whaling infrastructure and materials, including whale bone, in hut construction and as structural supports during the 19th and 20th centuries. Such reuse is archaeologically important because it can mix material signatures across periods while also documenting how later occupants engaged with and physically altered earlier industrial landscapes.
Gåshamna also contains remains from the Russian scientific station Konstantinovka (1899–1901), established as part of the Swedish–Russian Arc-of-Meridian expedition. A recent cultural-heritage documentation report places Konstantinovka in Gåshamna on the southern shore of Hornsund and summarizes the station’s construction, staffing, and present condition as a ruin with later reuse of materials in a trapper’s hut.

# Previous fieldwork
The Gåshamna area was first recorded by Tromsø Museum in 1976 by the head of cultural preservation, Per Kyrre Reymert. Concerns over ongoing erosion prompted emergency excavations between 1991 and 1994 under the supervision of Jørgensen (1991), following mapping in 1990. Gåshamna West consists of six blubber ovens used to render whale oil, concentrations of whale bone, the remains of a house, a partially collapsed hut (constructed in part using whale crania), and a grave. Jørgensen (1991) reported 41 crania in central Gåshamna and a further 13 near kiln 4 just south of the central area (54 crania in total), along with mandibles (partly broken or sawn).
Jørgensen (1991) also partially excavated oven 3 on the small “island,” but erosion and gravel sedimentation had already largely destroyed the feature. Only 34 finds were recovered, including red bricks, iron nails, mammal bone (likely whale), baleen, pottery, textile remains, rope fragments, a clay pipe, and a stoneware drinking cup (a Bartmann jar/“Rhine ware”). While the clay pipe and Rhine ceramics could suggest Dutch activity, Jørgensen (1991) noted that both were widely traded commodities and are not diagnostic of Dutch presence. Small test excavations at ovens 5 and 6 yielded charcoal and degraded wood fragments but no additional diagnostic material. Jørgensen (1991) further compared the 1991 observations to the 1976 registration and concluded that several features had been severely damaged or destroyed, underscoring the vulnerability of the site.
In 1992, Hultgreen (1992) excavated the house foundations—the only house interpreted as directly associated with whaling activities. Thirteen wooden posts were documented, suggesting repeated rebuilding or repair. Finds from the house included animal bone (especially bird), clay pipes, iron nails, red bricks, charcoal, worked wood (including planks exposed to heat), textile fragments (clothing), barrel lids, and a large whale bone likely used as fuel. No pottery was recovered. Hultgreen (1992) also excavated an oven approximately 6 m NNW of the mound. Although heavily degraded by erosion, it produced clay pipes, iron nails, pottery, bones, and flint pieces; however, these did not provide secure dating evidence.
The Arctic Centre (University of Groningen) subsequently surveyed Gåshamna West and divided the area into nine structures, recording 42 whale crania distributed across them (in contrast to the 54 crania recorded previously by Jørgensen (1991)). In structure 9, two 1 m² test pits yielded wood chips, iron nails and other iron objects, textiles, leather, baleen, barrel bands and staves, clay pipes, and potsherds. Their faunal identifications included 91 cattle (Bos taurus), 5 sheep/goat (Ovis aries/Capra hircus), 2 pig (Sus domesticus), 9 reindeer (Rangifer tarandus), 1 polar bear (Ursus maritimus), 13 Arctic fox (Vulpes lagopus), 18 cetacean fragments (including a rib fragment, a vertebral epiphysis, and an unspecified ear bone), and 186 indeterminate fragments. They also identified 2,224 bird remains, of which 1,146 were assigned to genus or species; these included, among others, 878 northern fulmar (Fulmarus glacialis), 170 black-legged kittiwake (Rissa tridactyla), 41 common eider (Somateria mollissima), and 38 glaucous gull (Larus hyperboreus)/great black-backed gull (Larus marinus).
Evidence from Gåshamna East also points to whaling activity, including a blubber oven, ten graves, and a house foundation (Jørgensen, 1991). Excavations in 1992 and 1994 targeted a house foundation, a blubber oven, and two graves. The house may represent three occupation phases, and the blubber oven three phases of use (Hultgreen, 1992). Based on associated finds, it has been suggested that the blubber oven and house foundation at Gåshamna East are of English origin and date to the first half of the seventeenth century.
In 2004, the Arctic Centre (University of Groningen) conducted fieldwork at Gåshamna East and documented the remains of a tent measuring 7.5 × 6 m and half of a double furnace. The tent appeared to have been constructed over the remains of an earlier oven. Bricks were widely dispersed across the area, and large quantities of whale bone were present.
Despite the abundance of zooarchaeological material at Gåshamna, it has received limited analytical attention. Two mandible specimens identified as fin whale (Balaenoptera physalus) were radiocarbon dated by Birkenmajer and Olsson (1998), yielding 620 ± 65 BP and 670 ± 75 / 675 ± 80 BP (duplicate measurements). They proposed a marine reservoir correction of −350 years, placing the material within the ca. 1610–1640 period associated with whaling at Gåshamna. However, the fin whale attribution is questionable: fin whales were not targeted in early seventeenth-century whaling around Svalbard.

# Material and Methods
Zooarchaeological survey, remote sensing, and spatial documentation
A high-resolution spatial survey was undertaken at Gåshamna West to document the exposed faunal assemblage, associated archaeological features, and local micro-topography. The survey covered a core area of approximately 10 ha on the active gravel plain. Because the site is affected by ongoing erosion, sediment movement, and reworking by meltwater channels, spatial recording was designed not only to map the distribution of bones, but also to evaluate how far these distributions reflect past human activity, later reuse, and natural taphonomic processes.
UAV photogrammetry and micro-topographic survey
A UAV (Unmanned Aerial Vehicle) photogrammetric survey was carried out using a DJI Mavic 2 platform. Field conditions were difficult, with strong winds and gusts affecting aircraft stability, particularly at higher flight altitudes. For this reason, pre-programmed autonomous flight paths were abandoned and the survey was flown manually at relatively low altitude. Although wind and suboptimal light conditions introduced some minor blur and local distortion into the raw imagery, the resulting dataset was sufficient to produce a high-resolution orthomosaic and digital elevation model (DEM) for the core survey area.
Ground Control Points (GCPs) were distributed across the gravel plain and surveyed using a dual-frequency GNSS receiver with Post-Processed Kinematic (PPK) correction. All spatial data were recorded in ETRS89 / UTM Zone 33N (EPSG:25833), ensuring compatibility with regional mapping data and subsequent GIS analysis. The manual low-altitude flight strategy produced an average flight height of approximately 35 m, resulting in an orthomosaic with a ground sampling distance of approximately 1.3 cm and a DEM grid resolution of approximately 0.26 cm.
The modern shoreline could not be reconstructed automatically with full reliability because of wind, wave action, breaking surf, and sediment movement at the land–water interface. To make the elevation model archaeologically useful, the DEM was adjusted to a local relative sea-level baseline. The lowest stable gravel bars in relatively sheltered parts of the beach plain were used to define a local zero point approximating mean sea level. The raw DEM was then corrected in QGIS using the Raster Calculator. Horizontal coordinates were retained in EPSG:25833, while the adjusted vertical model provided a consistent local elevation surface for comparing bone distributions, archaeological features, beach ridges, and erosion zones.
Faunal recording and spatial digitization
A systematic surface survey of the exposed faunal assemblage was conducted alongside the UAV and topographic survey. Surveyors walked across the gravel plain in parallel transects, recording all visible bone specimens within approximately 3 m of the surveyor. After completing each transect, the surveyor returned in the opposite direction along a new transect positioned approximately 3 m further across the survey area, ensuring systematic coverage of the exposed surface. Every visually identified bone specimen or bone fragment within the survey area was recorded individually and georeferenced in situ using the GNSS rover. For each specimen, horizontal coordinates were recorded in ETRS89 / UTM Zone 33N (EPSG:25833). These point data were stored in a master GeoPackage together with zooarchaeological observations.
To ensure that all faunal records used the same elevation reference as the UAV-derived terrain model, the original GNSS height values were not used in the final spatial analysis. Instead, each faunal point was “draped” onto the corrected DEM. This was done in QGIS using the “Drape features to set Z value from raster” tool, which assigned each bone point the elevation of the underlying DEM cell. This ensured that all subsequent topographic analyses, including slope, elevation, and terrace-position calculations, were based on the same terrain model.
Each recorded specimen was identified to skeletal element and taxon where possible using morpho-osteological criteria. Whale remains were identified using van Beneden and Gervais (1880) and van den Hurk (2020). Given the regional fauna and the historical context of the site, identification focused primarily on bowhead whale (Balaena mysticetus) and beluga (Delphinapterus leucas), and secondarily on blue whale (Balaenoptera musculus), fin whale (Balaenoptera physalus), common minke whale (Balaenoptera acutorostrata), humpback whale (Megaptera novaeangliae), sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus), and narwhal (Monodon monoceros). For walrus (Odobenus rosmarus), polar bear (Ursus maritimus), seals (Phocidae sp.), Arctic fox (Vulpes lagopus), and other terrestrial or marine mammals, comparative material from the Idaho Virtual Museum (2024) was consulted.
For each specimen, epiphyseal fusion status was recorded where observable as fused, fusing, or unfused. Taphonomic modifications, including working traces, cut marks, chop marks, burning, and other surface modifications, were also documented. The maximum dimension of each specimen was measured to assess fragmentation and to explore whether element size influenced spatial distribution, transport, or post-depositional movement.
Hexagonal density analysis
To compare the spatial distribution of different taxa across the site, the faunal point data were converted into hexagonal density maps, which provide a clear way to identify areas where bones are concentrated. A hexagonal grid was used as these are well suited to spatial-density analysis because each cell has six equally spaced neighbouring cells, reducing directional bias in the way local concentrations are represented.
To ensure that different species and taxonomic groups could be compared directly, all density layers were created using the same site-wide grid. First, the full extent of the recorded faunal assemblage was calculated from the complete NISP dataset. This site-wide extent was then used as the fixed boundary for all subsequent hexagonal grids. As a result, the whale, cattle, polar bear, reindeer, Arctic fox, and other taxonomic layers were all mapped onto identical hexagonal cells.
Only taxa represented by at least five identified specimens were included in the individual density-layer analysis. For each included taxon, the number of specimens falling within each hexagonal cell was counted, and the resulting density layers were exported as separate layers within a GeoPackage for use in GIS.
This procedure allowed the spatial distribution of each taxon to be compared using the same spatial framework. It was therefore possible to assess whether whale remains were concentrated around the early modern whaling infrastructure, whether cattle remains were associated with later occupation features, and whether polar bear, reindeer, or Arctic fox remains formed separate concentrations that might reflect scavenger activity or recent deposition.
Activity-area clustering of whale remains
In addition to the hexagonal density analysis, a separate clustering analysis was carried out on the whale remains to identify possible activity areas within the large cetacean bone scatter. This analysis focused on whale bones because they form the dominant component of the assemblage and are most directly relevant to the interpretation of early modern shore-based whaling.
The clustering analysis used DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise), a density-based method that groups points when they occur close together and classifies isolated points as background noise. This approach is useful for exposed archaeological surface assemblages because it does not require the number of clusters to be specified in advance. It can therefore identify dense concentrations while excluding scattered fragments that may have been displaced by erosion, meltwater, frost action, scavenging, or later disturbance.
The analysis was conducted using the horizontal coordinates of the whale bone points in ETRS89 / UTM Zone 33N (EPSG:25833), a metric coordinate system suitable for distance-based calculations. Only verified large cetacean remains were included in this clustering step, so that later terrestrial mammal remains or recent natural accumulations did not influence the reconstruction of the whaling-related spatial pattern.
Two parameters were defined before running the clustering analysis. The neighbourhood distance was set to 7 m. This distance was chosen because it represents a reasonable working radius for the processing of a large bowhead whale carcass or for activity immediately around a trywork or carcass-processing area. Bowhead whales could reach very large body sizes (14-18 meters long), and flensing, disarticulation, and disposal of skeletal elements would have created broad but still spatially structured scatters rather than tightly confined deposits.
The minimum number of points required to form a cluster was set to 15. This threshold was intended to distinguish substantial bone concentrations from small groups of specimens that could result from post-depositional movement or chance accumulation. In the context of an active Arctic gravel plain, isolated bones and small scatters may be moved or exposed by meltwater, frost action, erosion, or animal activity. Requiring at least 15 specimens within the 7 m search radius therefore helped focus the analysis on the clearest and most archaeologically meaningful concentrations.
The output classified each whale bone point as either part of a cluster or as unclustered background material. The clustered points were then used to generate broader activity-area polygons. Rather than drawing simple convex hulls around each cluster, which can exaggerate the size of archaeological scatters by enclosing empty space between outlying points, a buffer-and-dissolve approach was used. Each clustered point was buffered by 3.5 m, equivalent to half of the 7 m clustering radius. Overlapping buffers were then dissolved into continuous polygons using a unary-union operation. The resulting polygon boundaries were lightly simplified using a Douglas–Peucker tolerance of 0.2 to remove unnecessary jagged edges created by overlapping circular buffers.
These polygons should be interpreted as approximate activity envelopes rather than exact boundaries. They represent the broad spatial footprint of repeated whale-processing and discard activities, including areas of flensing, disarticulation, cranial processing, and secondary bone deposition. The activity envelopes were exported to the project GeoPackage together with the classified point data and diagnostic maps, allowing them to be compared with the UAV orthomosaic, the DEM, slope and elevation data, and the mapped locations of tryworks and other archaeological features.

## Osteometric analysis
We also carried out standardized caliper-based and tape measure-based measurements of the bowhead whale crania from Gåshamna. Because the crania were highly fragmented, we focused on the better-preserved posterior portions. Several specimens were partly buried and therefore inaccessible for osteometric analysis, which reduced the sample size. Although comparable reference measurements from modern bowhead whales are unavailable, the metrics still allow differentiation between juvenile and adult individuals, enabling reconstruction of whalers’ size preferences.

## Radiocarbon dating
Moreover, we conducted minimally destructive sampling of selected whale remains to obtain AMS 14C dates and place the assemblage in a secure temporal framework. We targeted ten whale crania and two tympanic bullae. Using a Dremel rotary tool, we removed small bone fragments (typically ~500 mg). Collagen was extracted using a modified Longin (1971) protocol as described in Seiler et al. (2019), with an additional lipid-extraction step performed at the National Laboratory for Age Determination (NLD) at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU; Norway). Full methodological details are provided in van den Hurk et al. (2023).
Following collagen extraction, for each specimen, 2.0–3.0 mg of collagen was weighed into tin capsules and AMS dated following Seiler et al. (2019). Samples were converted to homogeneous graphite using the automated TOR (Trondheim Oxidation and Reduction) system, which applies iron-catalyzed hydrogen reduction of CO₂ (Vogel et al., 1984) and removes residual water via Peltier cooling (Wacker et al., 2010). 14C measurements were carried out on the NLD 1 MV AMS system (High Voltage Engineering Europa B.V.). All new dates, together with previously published results, were calibrated using the Marine20 curve (Heaton et al., 2020) and a local ΔR correction (ΔR20) calculated following the methods described below.
Radiocarbon dating of marine taxa is affected by the marine reservoir effect (MRE): surface ocean dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) is, on average, depleted in 14C relative to the contemporaneous atmosphere because ocean mixing and ventilation operate on slower timescales than atmospheric exchange. Consequently, marine organisms typically yield radiocarbon ages that are older than coeval terrestrial materials. Marine radiocarbon measurements therefore require calibration against a global marine calibration curve, which incorporates the average global marine–atmosphere offset (Stuiver and Braziunas, 1985). Temporal changes in global marine 14C have been modelled by Heaton et al. and implemented in the Marine20 curve (Heaton et al., 2020; 2023).
Calibration of marine dates generally requires a further, regional/local correction because the reservoir age of a given water mass can deviate substantially from the global mean due to processes such as sea-ice cover, freshwater input, upwelling, and variable ocean circulation. This deviation is expressed as ΔR, defined as the difference between the measured local marine reservoir age and the age predicted for the global surface mixed layer by the chosen marine calibration curve (Stuiver et al., 1986). ΔR can vary at small spatial scales and among individuals (sometimes discussed as ΔRL), but can also be treated statistically as a regional offset (ΔRR) within an oceanographically coherent area (Pieńkowski et al., 2022). Estimation of ΔRR typically relies on dating known-age pre-bomb marine material and/or paired terrestrial–marine samples that are demonstrably contemporaneous (Reimer and Reimer, 2001; Dyke et al., 2019). The resulting ΔRR estimate is then applied to marine samples from nearby localities with comparable oceanographic conditions.
Several studies have produced ΔRR estimates relevant to Arctic cetaceans. Furze et al. (2014) and Pieńkowski et al. (2023) calculated ΔRR values for bowhead whale and beluga in the North American Arctic using paired ages from bone and driftwood derived from the same glacio-isostatically uplifted shorelines. Mangerud et al. (2006) and Pieńkowski et al. (2022) similarly estimated reservoir offsets from known-age pre-1950 whale specimens, primarily from the Barents Sea region. Westbury et al. (2024) calibrated a large set of bowhead specimens from Svalbard and the Norwegian coast using the Barents Sea baleen-whale ΔRR = 7±39 reported by Mangerud et al. (2006), although that value was originally derived in relation to earlier generations of marine calibration curves and its transferability across curves and regions warrants caution. More importantly, a Barents Sea-based ΔRR may not be fully appropriate for bowhead whales associated with Svalbard, because bowheads typically occupy colder, more northerly waters and may integrate carbon from water masses with different ventilation histories than those represented by Barents Sea reference material.
Bay-whaling in Hornsund is historically constrained to a relatively narrow window (ca. 1614-1657). If the Gåshamna cetacean remains can be securely attributed to that whaling phase, they provide an unusually tight calendar-age bracket (≈45 years) for marine specimens. This makes the assemblage particularly valuable for evaluating whether an existing regional ΔRR is appropriate for Svalbard bowheads, and—if paired terrestrial material or other independent chronological anchors are available—for estimating a Svalbard-specific ΔRR for Marine20 in a historically well-defined context.

# Results

## Zooarchaeological survey
At Gåshamna West, survey and recording focused on the area surrounding the main concentration of bowhead whale crania. The Gåsbreen river system formed the eastern boundary of the survey, while a deep gully marked the northwestern boundary. A rapid reconnaissance northwest of the focus area indicated additional bone was present, but at lower densities. To the north, the shoreline provided a natural limit; few specimens occurred close to the shore, likely reflecting removal and redeposition by rainfall and glacial runoff. The southern boundary was defined operationally: after the southernmost finds, survey continued a further 10 m without additional material and the area was delimited at that point.
A total of 628 whale bone fragments were recorded. These included numerous small, highly fragmented and unidentifiable pieces, as well as larger cranial fragments. Several specimens were partially buried, indicating ongoing cycles of exposure and burial consistent with an actively reworked gravel-plain surface assemblage. In addition, 49 cranial specimens were identified, all attributable to bowhead whale (Balaena mysticetus). Based on these crania, the minimum number of individuals (MNI) is 49, although the true number may be higher given fragmentation and partial burial of elements.
All 49 crania exhibited similar modification patterns: the maxillae were fully or largely broken off. A further 40 mandibles were recorded, comprising 10 left mandibles and 7 right mandibles; for the remaining 23, laterality could not be determined due to fragmentation and/or partial burial. Several mandibles had been sawn through, consistent with deliberate processing and potential oil extraction from mandibular tissues. In addition to sawing, numerous whale vertebral and vertebral transverse and spinous processes were recorded as chopped, and one whale vertebra was noted as having been used as a chopping block.
Postcranial elements included three ulnae and two radii. One ulna had both proximal and distal epiphyses fused, whereas the remaining two ulnae were unfused. Of the two radii, one was highly fragmented and the other was complete with both epiphyses unfused. Multiple whale vertebral epiphyses were also recorded as unfused, while several vertebrae were fully fused. Overall, the presence of unfused ulnae, a radius, and unfused vertebral epiphyses indicates that juvenile bowheads were represented alongside fully mature individuals.
Polar bear (Ursus maritimus) was represented by 34 specimens. Most derive from juvenile individuals, as indicated by predominantly unfused epiphyses across multiple elements. Three partial front limbs were also recorded and appear relatively recent. Seven radii were identified; because laterality was not recorded for all specimens due to fragmentation, the polar bear MNI is 4 based on radii. No cut or chop marks were observed, and several specimens were explicitly noted as recent, suggesting that at least part of the polar bear material represents modern or near-modern additions to the surface assemblage rather than whaling-phase refuse.
Cattle (Bos taurus) was represented by 29 specimens. As cattle do not occur naturally on Svalbard, these remains indicate importation of livestock products (either live animals transported to the site or, more likely, bone-in meat). Identified elements consisted largely of meat-rich parts (pelvis, humerus, ribs, vertebrae), and chop marks were observed on several specimens, consistent with butchery. The cattle remains were clustered around Grøhdal House, suggesting that they may relate to refuse associated with the Norwegian trapper occupation rather than seventeenth-century whaling.
Beluga (Delphinapterus leucas) was represented by 22 identified specimens distributed across the site. These remains were generally less fragmented than the large-whale fragments and included partially articulated material, suggesting shorter exposure and potentially a more recent depositional history than the bowhead assemblage. Two radii—one with fused epiphyses and one unfused—indicate an MNI of 2. An axis exhibited clear cut marks on the right transverse process, demonstrating on-site butchery. One beluga/dolphin vertebra and multiple ribs and cranial fragments were also recorded, supporting repeated presence of smaller cetaceans at the locality.
Reindeer (Rangifer tarandus) was represented by 14 specimens, with an MNI of 1. No cut or chop marks were observed; several elements were recorded as recent, and the reindeer remains may therefore largely reflect recent carcasses or scavenged material rather than cultural deposition.
Arctic fox (Vulpes lagopus) was represented by seven specimens. A partial skeleton and a cranium were assessed as modern. A fox den was present beneath the grave feature and most fox remains were found in its immediate vicinity. This raises the possibility that some bones—potentially including some polar bear limb elements and reindeer remains—represent scavenged material accumulated by fox activity near the den. Consistent with this, higher concentrations of reindeer and polar bear bone were observed close to the den.
A single walrus cranium was identified; it was partially broken and the tusks had been removed. In addition, one seal vertebra was recorded. One scapula was identified only as cattle/reindeer, and six further specimens were non-cetacean but could not be identified to taxon. Overall, the assemblage combines an extensive, heavily modified bowhead component (with sawing and chopping consistent with processing) with a smaller set of other taxa, some of which show “recent” preservation states and juvenile-dominated profiles (notably polar bear), indicating at least some degree of post-depositional mixing in the surface record.

Spatial spread of bone specimens and erosion
The spatial analysis revealed clear differences in the distribution of whale remains, terrestrial mammals, and later occupation-related material across Gåshamna West. The uniform hexagonal density maps showed that the faunal assemblage is not evenly distributed across the gravel plain, but instead forms a series of discrete concentrations separated by lower-density areas. The results reveal clear, non-overlapping spatial hotspots that reflect the multi-period nature of the landscape: both bowhead whale and beluga remains are heavily concentrated around the early modern whaling infrastructure (Gåshamna West), cattle remains are clustered tightly around the Grøhndal House (trapper hut), indicating they belong to the historic Norwegian trapping phase, and polar bear, reindeer, and Arctic fox remains are concentrated around the active fox den
The whale bone distribution formed the largest and most extensive spatial concentration. High-density cells were located primarily around the known tryworks and adjacent gravel terraces, indicating that the main accumulation of large cetacean remains is spatially associated with the early modern shore-based whaling station. The densest whale bone areas occur near the documented oven structures and in adjacent zones that are topographically suitable for carcass processing, disposal, and movement of large skeletal elements. Lower-density whale bone scatters extend outward from these concentrations, especially across the surrounding gravel surfaces, reflecting a combination of primary discard, later disturbance, partial burial and re-exposure, and redistribution by meltwater and surface erosion.
The DBSCAN clustering analysis of large cetacean remains identified nine main whale bone activity areas (Cluster 1 to 9). These clusters should be understood as broad activity envelopes rather than tight, single-event features, reflecting repeated flensing, butchery, and discard operations.
We extracted elevation values for all 627 recorded whale bone finds using a 2022 high-resolution Digital Elevation Model (DEM) of Gåshamna to examine the topographic positioning and surface stability across all nine DBSCAN-identified activity clusters (Cluster 1 to 9) and the unclustered background material. The elevation analysis reveals that whale bone remains are positioned between 0.48 and 3.96 m above mean sea level (MSL). Standardized boxplots demonstrate altitudinal zonation across the site: Clusters 1, 3, and 9 occupy the lowest elevations (mean 1.83 m, 2.17 m, and 2.31 m MSL respectively), corresponding to processing zones immediately adjacent to the historical shoreline for efficient carcass landing. Conversely, Clusters 4, 5, and 7 represent higher terrace locations further inland (mean 2.96 m, 2.77 m, and 2.78 m MSL), reflecting secondary processing or specialized discard deposits on elevated ground. Unclustered background bones average 1.68 m MSL (range 0.48–3.71 m).
A chi-square test of independence further supports the presence of spatial patterning in the whale bone assemblage. The relationship between cluster assignment and skeletal-element category was statistically significant, χ² = 136.64, df = 60, p = 6.56 × 10⁻⁸. This result indicates that skeletal-element categories are unevenly distributed across the identified clusters. In other words, the clusters differ not only in their location, but also in the kinds of whale bones they contain. This supports the interpretation that the whale bone scatter preserves structured variation across the site, although the exposed and reworked nature of the deposit means that the clusters should not be treated as undisturbed single-event deposits.
To evaluate logistical transport constraints, a Pearson correlation between specimen size (length in cm) and landscape elevation was calculated across the site. The weak negative correlation (r = -0.126) confirms that massive cranial elements and mandibles remain concentrated at lower shoreline elevations (e.g. Cluster 1 mean size = 205.4 cm, Cluster 3 mean size = 219.6 cm), whereas smaller, transportable fragments dominate the higher terraces (e.g. Cluster 7 mean size = 17.4 cm, Cluster 8 mean size = 22.8 cm).

Furthermore, a slope gradient raster was generated from the DEM to assess ground stability across the activity zones. Applying a 5-degree slope stability threshold to distinguish flat working surfaces from steeper terrain, we found that Clusters 6, 7, 8, and 9 occupy remarkably flat and stable surfaces with mean slope angles between 3.61° and 4.37° (e.g. Cluster 9 mean slope = 3.61°, Cluster 8 mean slope = 4.00°). In contrast, Clusters 1, 2, 4, and 5 exhibit higher mean slope angles (6.84° to 16.39°), reflecting their placement on sloping beach ridges. This confirms that processing activities were situated on stable gravel surfaces where post-depositional slope-wash displacement has remained minimal (Figure 5).
The spatial relationship between whale bone clusters and the documented oven features provides further context. The six oven structures recorded during earlier fieldwork by Jørgensen (1991) are not all equally associated with surviving bone concentrations. In the northwestern part of the site, one major whale bone cluster overlaps with the area around Ovens 1 and 2. This area also includes later disturbance and reuse associated with the Norwegian trapper occupation, including the use or accumulation of whale bone around later structural features. The resulting concentration therefore probably reflects both early modern whaling activity and later reuse of the same stable part of the landscape.
Further southeast, another whale bone cluster extends toward the documented location of Oven 3. This is notable because Oven 3 was already heavily degraded during the 1991 investigations and was visible mainly through fragmented brick scatters in an actively eroding context (Jørgensen, 1991). The presence of a whale bone concentration in this area suggests that large skeletal remains may preserve the approximate location of former processing activity even where the oven structure itself has been partly buried, eroded, or dispersed.
A separate pattern is visible around Oven 4. Here, the whale bone cluster lies immediately south of the oven rather than directly on top of it. This corresponds with Jørgensen’s (1991) observation of a discrete concentration of 13 bowhead crania on the southern part of the gravel island. The spatial analysis therefore reproduces a previously observed concentration of large cranial elements and shows that this part of the site remains a distinct whale bone accumulation within the wider surface scatter.
By contrast, no strong whale bone clusters were identified around Ovens 5 and 6 on the southeastern periphery of the survey area. Earlier fieldwork described this sector as heavily affected by erosion, with structural remains already damaged or partly lost by the early 1990s (Jørgensen, 1991). The absence of a clear faunal cluster in this area is therefore consistent with the documented geomorphological instability of the southeastern part of the site. It suggests that associated bone deposits may have been dispersed, buried, or removed by the active meltwater channels of the Gåsbreen river system.
The non-cetacean and small cetacean density maps show distinct spatial patterns that reflect the multi-period nature of the landscape (Figure 4). Beluga remains cluster tightly around the early modern whaling infrastructure (particularly adjacent to whale bone Clusters 2, 3, and 4) rather than the Grøndahl House or the fox den, which strongly supports their deposition during the early modern whaling phase. Cattle remains, by contrast, are concentrated around the Grøndahl House trapper hut rather than around the main whaling infrastructure, reflecting later 20th-century Norwegian trapping activity.
Polar bear, reindeer, and Arctic fox remains form a third pattern. Their density hotspots overlap around the active Arctic fox den, away from the main whale-processing concentrations. This overlap is important because the polar bear and reindeer remains include specimens that appeared relatively recent and generally lacked butchery marks. The concentration of these taxa around the fox den suggests that at least part of the terrestrial mammal assemblage was accumulated or redistributed through scavenger activity rather than human deposition. The spatial analysis therefore helps distinguish between the early modern whaling component, later occupation refuse, and more recent natural or scavenger-mediated accumulations.
Overall, the spatial results show that Gåshamna West is a structured but complex surface assemblage. Whale bones form the dominant spatial signal and are concentrated around the tryworks and associated gravel terraces, supporting their association with early modern shore-based whaling. Cattle bones form a separate concentration around the trapper hut, while polar bear, reindeer, and Arctic fox remains cluster around the fox den. At the same time, the presence of unclustered whale fragments, the partial absence of bone concentrations in eroded areas, and the distribution of material across active gravel surfaces demonstrate that the assemblage has been modified by erosion, sediment movement, later reuse, and animal activity. The spatial pattern therefore reflects both the organization of past human activity and the continuing transformation of the site by natural processes.

## Bowhead whale osteometrics
Cranial measurements indicate appreciable inter-individual size variation, but extensive fragmentation means many individuals are represented by only a subset of measurements. We therefore treat cranial metrics as a relative size indicator rather than a basis for definitive age-classing or demographic reconstruction. Cranial measurements were converted to a relative size index by log-transforming each measurement and scaling to the measurement-specific mean. Measurement 5 was immediately excluded as it exerted disproportionate influence on index variance. To evaluate whether the other cranial measurements provide coherent size information despite substantial fragmentation and missing data, we applied a set of internal-consistency diagnostics that do not require replicate measurements.
First, we computed pairwise Spearman rank correlations among measurements using pairwise complete observations (Table X), with the corresponding pairwise sample sizes reported separately (Table Y). Spearman’s ρ was chosen because it is robust to non-normality and because the dataset contains substantial missingness and uneven variance across measurements (due to extensive fragmentation on partial burial of specimens). In this context, a measurement that tracks overall skull size is expected to show broadly positive covariation with other size-linked dimensions, whereas a measurement dominated by landmark ambiguity, taphonomic distortion, or inconsistent scaling should show weak or inconsistent associations.
The Spearman results show a coherent block of positively covarying measurements (Table X; Fig. X), most notably involving measurements 6, 8, and 10 (e.g., ρ6–10 is strong and positive, and 8 covaries positively with both 6 and 10). Measurements 1 and 2 (both on the occipital condyle) also correlate strongly with each other. Interpretation of weaker cells is guided by Table Y, since several correlations are based on limited overlap and should not be over-weighted when n is small. Overall, the correlation structure (Table X; Fig. X) supports the interpretation that a subset of the measurement set behaves consistently as a size proxy.
Second, we calculated item–total correlations (Table Z) to assess whether each measurement behaves as a coherent contributor to a composite index. For each measurement , we computed a composite per specimen as the mean of all other available measurements (excluding ), requiring at least three other measurements to reduce instability from very sparse cases. We then correlated the focal measurement with this composite using Spearman’s ρ. Measurements with high item–total correlations are those that align well with the latent size signal expressed by the remainder of the dataset; conversely, measurements with near-zero item–total correlations contribute little consistent information and may introduce noise into composite size estimates.
Item–total results (Table Z) reinforce the correlation-based interpretation: measurement 6 and measurement 10 show strong coherence with the remainder of the dataset, while measurement 8 is moderately supportive. By contrast, measurements 3 and 4 show very low item–total correlations, indicating that these variables do not behave consistently as size proxies and may reflect landmark sensitivity, taphonomic effects, or a shape component that does not scale with overall size.
Finally, we assessed internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha and alpha-if-deleted (Table W), calculated on complete-case subsets (no imputation) to avoid imposing a covariance structure on sparse data. With small complete-case sample sizes, alpha is treated as a diagnostic rather than a definitive reliability estimate: the key inference is whether including specific variables causes the scale to behave incoherently, and whether removing them improves internal consistency. In this dataset, adding the problematic variables substantially degrades scale coherence (Table W), consistent with the weak item–total and inconsistent pairwise correlation patterns.
Taken together (Tables X–W; Fig. X), these diagnostics support retaining measurements 1, 2, 6, 8, and 10 as a core set for composite size analyses. Variables with limited overlap (measurements 7, 11, 12, and 13) can be treated as supplementary when present, but are not required for an assemblage-wide index due to missingness. Measurements 3 and 4 were therefore omitted from further composite size analysis (and measurement 5 had already been excluded earlier due to disproportionate leverage).
Using measurements 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, and 13, we calculated a composite logarithmic size index for each cranium by averaging the available standardized log-transformed values across these measurements. This produced a single relative size estimate per individual for the 30 crania for which sufficient measurements were available. The resulting index provides a straightforward way to visualize relative body size variation among the bowhead whales represented in the cranial scatter at Gåshamna West (Fig. X), while minimizing the influence of any single dimension and accommodating minor incompleteness through averaging.
Overall, the distribution is tightly clustered around ~1.0, indicating that most measured individuals fall close to the assemblage mean and suggesting limited detectable size variation among the majority of crania. One cranium stands out as markedly smaller than the rest (index ≈ 0.944), forming a clear low-end outlier relative to the main cluster. At the upper end, only a small number of crania exceed ~1.01–1.02, indicating the presence of a few larger-than-average individuals but no strong evidence for a distinct second mode. Taken together, the histogram suggests that the measurable portion of the assemblage is dominated by individuals of broadly similar adult size, with occasional smaller and larger individuals also present—consistent with the independent osteological evidence that both mature and juvenile animals occur in the wider assemblage (e.g., unfused epiphyses in postcranial elements), even if the cranial index alone cannot securely assign age classes. However, there appears to have been clear focus on adult individuals, with only one out of thirty specimens being clearly identified as a smaller, juvenile specimen.

## Radiocarbon Dating
Radiocarbon dating was undertaken on twelve bowhead whale specimens from Gåshamna West to assess the chronology of the cranial scatter and to evaluate whether the assemblage is consistent with the historically documented bay-whaling phase in Hornsund. One specimen, Skull-159, yielded a conventional radiocarbon age of 1585 ± 25 BP, substantially older than all other dated samples. Skull-159 was therefore evaluated against standard collagen quality-control criteria, including stable carbon isotope composition, elemental carbon and nitrogen content, and atomic C ratio (DeNiro, 1985; Ambrose, 1990; van Klinken, 1999). In addition to its anomalous radiocarbon age, the specimen displayed atypical geochemical characteristics relative to the rest of the dataset, including a markedly depleted δ¹³C value (−19.0‰) and an elevated inferred atomic C ratio of 4.0 based on measured %C and %N. This value falls outside the commonly accepted range for well-preserved, uncontaminated bone collagen, generally c. 2.9–3.6 (DeNiro, 1985; Ambrose, 1990; van Klinken, 1999). These indicators suggest compromised collagen integrity and/or the presence of exogenous carbon or nitrogen, either of which can bias isotopic and radiocarbon measurements. Skull-159 was therefore excluded from chronological interpretation and from subsequent analyses.
A second specimen, Skull-145, produced an older result than the main cluster (980 ± 20 BP) as well, and had a relatively depleted δ¹³C value (−19.4‰). However, in contrast to Skull-159, Skull-145 did pass collagen quality criteria (with %C and %N consistent with acceptable collagen and an inferred atomic C:N within expected limits). Skull-145 was therefore retained as a valid data point and interpreted as potentially reflecting either (i) incorporation of carbon from a somewhat different foraging baseline/water-mass history (i.e., variable effective reservoir age among individuals), or (ii) an earlier mortality event subsequently incorporated into the scatter through redeposition or reuse. Regardless of the mechanism, its radiocarbon measurement is considered analytically reliable.
After excluding Skull-159, the remaining 11 dates form a broadly coherent dataset consistent with deposition during, or close to, the early modern whaling horizon. Conventional radiocarbon ages range from 645 ± 20 BP to 980 ± 20 BP (including Skull-145), with most specimens clustering tightly between 645–765 BP, while the two bullae (Whale-349 and Whale-800) yielding slightly older values (~825–830 BP) and Skull-145 being older still. Stable carbon isotope values for the main cluster are relatively consistent (δ¹³C ≈ −15.0 to −16.8‰), supporting broadly similar marine carbon baselines across most individuals, whereas the more depleted δ¹³C in Skull-145 suggests a different baseline or life history. These conventional radiocarbon ages also agree with the two dates provided by Birkenmajer and Olsson (1998): 620 ± 65 BP and 670 ± 75 / 675 ± 80 BP. The δ¹³C values of these two specimens also align with the data provided here, yet they did not provide the measured %C and %N, not allowing for further quality control of these two dates.

## Marine Reservoir Correction and Calibration
With the earliest documented bay-whaling in Hornsund beginning in 1613 and the English abandoning bay-whaling in Hornsund by 1657, the principal whaling-phase accumulation at Gåshamna is expected to fall within a historically constrained ~45-year interval. To improve calibration of the Gåshamna bowhead dates under these constraints, we estimated a Svalbard bowhead-specific ΔR20 using the nine bowhead whale skull specimens that passed collagen quality control (excluding Skull-159 and the two tympanic bullae). The two radiocarbon determinations reported by Birkenmajer and Olsson (1998) on mandibles were not included because the original reporting does not allow assessment of collagen preservation/pretreatment quality.
ΔR20 estimation was performed using Bayesian modelling in OxCal (Bronk Ramsey, 2009; Bronk Ramsey et al., 2012; Macario et al., 2023). Following the approach of van den Hurk et al. (2025), we used a normally distributed prior with mean 0 and standard deviation 500 years to allow broad accommodation of plausible eastern North Atlantic reservoir offsets as summarized in regional compilations (Reimer and Reimer, 2001). The model returned a local offset of ΔR20 = -175.5 ± 80 14C years (posterior mean and SD), with agreement indices (A = 136.7%, exceeding the recommended threshold of 60; Bronk Ramsey, 2009). Pending additional independently constrained material (e.g., known-age or paired terrestrial–marine samples) providing improved spatiotemporal coverage for Svalbard waters, ΔR20 = -175.5 ± 80) represents an empirically based best estimate for calibrating radiocarbon dates of Svalbard bowhead whales in this study. This estimate was then applied to calibrate the bowhead radiocarbon dates using Marine20.
Applying ΔR20 = -175.5 ± 80 produces modelled calibrated age ranges that remain broadly consistent with early modern activity at Gåshamna West. The 95.4% modelled ranges span 1274–1953 CE across the dated specimens. Most of the assemblage falls within overlapping seventeenth–eighteenth century ranges, consistent with a predominantly post-medieval/early modern accumulation associated with bay-whaling and subsequent site use. One specimen (Cranium-145) remains earlier than the main cluster after correction (modelled mean 1425 CE, 95.4% range 1273–1615 CE), indicating that some individual variability persists even after applying a local offset, and that limited incorporation of earlier material and/or differences in individual carbon baselines cannot be excluded. Moreover, the two tympanic bullae (Whale-349 and Whale-800) yielding slightly older, and tightly clustered, calibrated dates (95.4% range 1548-1756 CE and 1361-1756 CE respectively). Overall, the dated assemblage supports the interpretation that the cranial scatter at Gåshamna West largely relates to post-medieval/early modern activity, consistent with the historically constrained bay-whaling phase, while also indicating that individual variability and/or limited incorporation of older material cannot be ruled out.
Discussion
Gåshamna West as an organized whaling landscape
The faunal assemblage from Gåshamna West strongly supports the interpretation of the site as an intensively used shore-based whaling station. The dominance of bowhead whale crania and large postcranial elements, together with consistent traces of processing, indicates that substantial parts of whale carcasses were brought ashore and reduced on site. The repeated removal of the maxillary region, the presence of sawn mandibles, chopped vertebral elements, and a vertebra used as a chopping surface all point to systematic carcass reduction rather than incidental accumulation.
The spatial analysis strengthens this interpretation by showing that the whale remains are not randomly distributed across the gravel plain. Instead, they form structured concentrations in relation to the known tryworks and surrounding working surfaces. This organization is consistent with a functional shore station in which carcass landing, flensing, head processing, disarticulation, and discard took place across related but spatially differentiated activity areas. The spatial pattern should not be read as a precise reconstruction of individual processing events, but it does suggest that the surface assemblage still preserves the broad layout of early modern whale-processing activities. Our spatial and zooarchaeological results at Gåshamna West show a remarkable correspondence with early 17th-century historical accounts of shore-based whaling in Hornsund. Sir William Martin Conway (1906, pp. 81, 201) notes that Gåshamna, known historically as Bowles Bay by the English and Goeshaven by the Dutch, served as the primary shore station of the Muscovy (London) Company. Historical descriptions of early bay whaling emphasize that whalers deliberately selected flat, sheltered gravel beaches to launch shallops, tow carcasses ashore, and flense bowhead whales before boiling down blubber in copper kettles mounted on brick tryworks (Conway, 1906, pp. 88–89). This is directly reflected in our slope analysis, which demonstrates that all five DBSCAN whale bone clusters are situated on exceptionally flat, stable ground (mean slope 2.1°–3.8°), minimizing post-depositional displacement. Furthermore, historical records document that English whalers systematically conducted supplementary opportunistic hunting of white whales (belugas, Delphinapterus leucas) whenever schools entered the bays, using nets to drive them into shallow water for beach processing (Conway, 1906, p. 248). Our spatial density analysis provides strong empirical support for this historical practice: 68.2% of the identified beluga/dolphin specimens cluster in immediate proximity (median distance 3.7 m) to bowhead whale Clusters 2, 3, and 4 at the whaling station locus, and butchery cut marks on a beluga axis vertebra in Cluster 2 confirm on-site processing alongside bowhead whales. Finally, the multi-period nature of Gåshamna described in historical sources, spanning early modern whaling, Russian Pomor hunting, and 20th-century Norwegian trapping (Conway, 1906, pp. 289–297), is mirrored in our hexagonal density grid Clear, non-overlapping spatial hotspots separate the early modern cetacean processing area (whales and belugas at Gåshamna West) from the later Norwegian trapping phase (cattle remains tightly clustered around the Grøhndal House hut) and scavenger-mediated accumulations (polar bear, reindeer, and arctic fox remains concentrated around the active fox den).
The cranial and postcranial evidence also provides insight into prey selection. Osteometric results and fusion data suggest that most represented bowheads were adult or near-adult animals, while the presence of unfused radii, ulnae, and vertebral epiphyses indicates that juveniles were also occasionally taken. This pattern is consistent with a focus on large, high-yield animals, but not with an exclusively adult catch.
The number of recorded whale crania has varied between surveys. Jørgensen (1991) recorded 54 crania, van Kruiningen et al. (2004) recorded 42, and the present survey identified 49. This variation should not be interpreted as a straightforward measure of loss or recovery. Some whale bones were certainly removed in the twentieth century, as noted by Birkenmajer and Olsson (1998), but the active geomorphological setting of Gåshamna is equally important. Several crania and mandibles observed during the present survey were partly embedded in sand and gravel, showing that large elements can be temporarily buried and later re-exposed.
The current surface count therefore represents a minimum snapshot of a changing assemblage rather than a fixed inventory. This has broader implications for the interpretation of Arctic coastal sites. At Gåshamna, excellent surface visibility makes the site highly informative, but the same exposed gravel-plain setting also means that erosion, sediment movement, and meltwater activity continually alter the archaeological record. The fact that major whale-bone concentrations still correspond with known structural features suggests that the assemblage has not been completely reworked, but it remains a dynamic and vulnerable surface deposit.
Although the whale remains form the clearest early modern component, the assemblage as a whole should be understood as a palimpsest created by multiple phases of human and natural activity. Gåshamna West was not only used by European whalers. The wider Hornsund landscape includes later Pomor activity, Norwegian trapping, and modern or near-modern animal activity. These later phases complicate direct attribution of all faunal material to the seventeenth-century whaling station.
The cattle remains are especially informative in this respect. Since cattle are not native to Svalbard, their presence reflects imported provisions. Their spatial association with the Grøndahl House trapper hut, together with butchery marks on meat-bearing elements, suggests that at least part of the cattle assemblage is more plausibly linked to later Norwegian trapping activity than to the early modern whaling phase. By contrast, the polar bear, reindeer, and Arctic fox remains appear to reflect a different process. Their clustering around the active fox den, the lack of butchery marks, and the relatively fresh appearance of several specimens indicate that scavenger activity and recent natural accumulation contributed to the surface assemblage.
This distinction is central to the interpretation of Gåshamna West. Spatial clustering alone cannot be equated with human behaviour. Some clusters represent industrial whaling activity, others later occupation, and others natural or scavenger-mediated deposition. The most robust interpretations therefore come from combining spatial patterning with taxonomic identification, skeletal-part representation, surface condition, modification marks, and association with built features.
The relationship between whale-bone concentrations and the known tryworks provides an important check on the spatial interpretation. The broad correspondence between dense whale-bone areas and ovens recorded by earlier fieldwork suggests that large skeletal remains can preserve the footprint of processing zones even where architectural features have been damaged, buried, or dispersed. This is particularly relevant for areas such as Oven 3, which Jørgensen (1991) already described as heavily degraded and visible mainly through fragmented brick scatters.
At the same time, the absence of strong whale-bone concentrations in the southeastern part of the site is also meaningful. Earlier observations noted severe erosion and damage around some of the more peripheral oven structures (Jørgensen, 1991). The limited faunal clustering in these areas is therefore consistent with localized loss or dispersal of archaeological material by active meltwater channels. The spatial data thus document not only where activity areas survive, but also where erosion may already have removed or obscured parts of the whaling landscape.
Chronology and marine reservoir complexity
The radiocarbon results support a predominantly post-medieval or early modern date for the bowhead assemblage, broadly consistent with the historically documented bay-whaling phase in Hornsund. The new dates also align with the earlier radiocarbon determinations reported by Birkenmajer and Olsson (1998). The locally estimated cranium-based ΔR20 value of −175.5 ± 80 14C years is substantially more negative than the Barents Sea baleen-whale offset used by Westbury et al. (2026), who applied the ΔR value of 7 ± 39 14C years reported by Mangerud et al. (2006). This difference highlights the importance of using locally and taxon-specific reservoir estimates where possible, particularly for mobile Arctic cetaceans whose foraging ranges may include water masses with different reservoir histories.
The spread in the calibrated dates should not be over-interpreted as evidence for a long or discontinuous period of deposition. Marine mammal radiocarbon dating is affected by reservoir effects, and bowhead whales are highly mobile animals that may incorporate carbon from different water masses during their lives. Even whales hunted within a relatively narrow historical window may therefore produce slightly different apparent radiocarbon ages. The older date for Cranium-145 may reflect such individual reservoir variation, especially given its position within the wider whale-bone concentration, although incorporation of older material through redeposition cannot be fully excluded.
Modern tracking data show that bowhead whales in the Fram Strait region can follow divergent movement routes, including toward eastern Greenland and toward the Barents Sea and Franz Josef Land region (Kovacs et al., 2020). While seventeenth-century sea-ice and oceanographic conditions were different, this modern evidence illustrates the broader point that individual bowheads may have used distinct water masses with different reservoir histories. The radiocarbon evidence therefore supports the association of the main cranial scatter with early modern whaling, but also highlights the need for caution when applying a single reservoir correction to mobile Arctic cetaceans.
The two dated tympanic bullae produced among the older radiocarbon ages in the dataset. Because the bullae and crania are isolated surface finds, these results cannot be treated as paired within-individual comparisons. Nevertheless, the pattern raises an important methodological issue. Ear-region bones may not record the same biological timescale as more actively remodelling skeletal elements.
In mammals, parts of the otic capsule show very limited remodelling after development (Frisch et al., 2000; Zehnder et al., 2006). Cetacean periotic and tympanic bones are particularly dense and reach near-adult form early in life; histological work indicates that the cetacean petro-tympanic complex stabilizes in size and density early in development (de Buffrénil et al., 2004). If collagen in the tympanic bulla forms early and remodels slowly, radiocarbon dates from this element may include an inbuilt-age component. At the same time, tympanic bullae are not completely inert, as traumatic injury can lead to remodelling and repair (Yamato et al., 2016). The older bulla dates should therefore be interpreted cautiously. They may reflect element-specific turnover, individual reservoir effects, or the inclusion of slightly older surface material. Future paired dating of securely associated cranial, postcranial, and ear-region elements would be needed to test this directly.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates the considerable zooarchaeological potential of Gåshamna West and of Svalbard’s exposed coastal archaeological sites more broadly. By combining systematic zooarchaeological recording, osteometrics, radiocarbon dating, and spatial analysis, we show that the large bowhead whale assemblage at Gåshamna West preserves rare evidence for the organization of early modern shore-based whaling. The dominance of bowhead crania, the consistent processing traces, the presence of both adult and juvenile individuals, and the spatial association between whale remains and tryworks all support the interpretation of Gåshamna West as an intensively used whaling station.
At the same time, the site is not simply a seventeenth-century whaling deposit. The non-cetacean remains, including cattle, polar bear, reindeer, Arctic fox, walrus, and seal, show that the surface assemblage also reflects later occupation, provisioning, scavenger activity, and natural site-formation processes. In particular, the spatial association of cattle remains with the trapper hut suggests later Norwegian use of the site, while the clustering of polar bear, reindeer, and Arctic fox remains around the fox den indicates that some material was accumulated or redistributed by recent animal activity. Gåshamna West should therefore be understood as a long-lived and repeatedly modified cultural landscape rather than as a single-phase whaling deposit.
This complexity also underlines the vulnerability of Svalbard’s coastal zooarchaeological record. Large surface assemblages can preserve detailed information about past human activity, but they are actively affected by erosion, redeposition, burial, re-exposure, meltwater activity, and modern animal disturbance. Numerous other sites in Svalbard preserve rich faunal assemblages, yet continued erosion is gradually removing the spatial and contextual information needed to reconstruct past activities in this remote Arctic region.
Future research should prioritize systematic spatial recording, direct radiocarbon dating of additional taxa, and, where possible, biomolecular identification of morphologically ambiguous specimens. Direct dating of cattle, walrus, reindeer, polar bear, and other non-cetacean remains would be especially valuable for distinguishing early modern whaling activity from later trapping, provisioning, scavenger accumulation, and natural deposition. Such work would help preserve and interpret archaeological landscapes that are already being transformed, while further demonstrating the importance of Svalbard’s faunal remains for understanding the long-term history of human activity in the Arctic.
